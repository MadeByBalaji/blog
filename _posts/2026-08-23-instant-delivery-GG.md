## Why I Think We Should Stop Buying From Instant Delivery Services

It was a rainy evening around 7 PM, and I was on the road heading to an eatery for dinner. The roads were wet, traffic was messy, and everyone seemed to be trying to get somewhere. Then I saw a delivery agent riding an Activa at what looked like more than 50 km/h on the wrong side of a one-way road. A few moments later, there was an accident. His Activa was damaged badly enough that it was no longer rideable.

I don't know how much he was earning from that particular delivery, but let's assume he was trying to earn another ₹30–₹50. Now imagine trying to earn ₹30–₹50 and ending up with a ₹4,000–₹6,000 repair bill. That is without even considering the injuries he may have suffered. Seeing that accident made me think about the entire instant-delivery culture and the pressure it places on delivery workers.

### Are We Really Saving Time?

We have become so comfortable with getting almost anything delivered within 10, 20 or 30 minutes that we sometimes forget what makes those few minutes possible. Someone has to ride through traffic, deal with the rain, navigate crowded roads and meet the delivery deadline. As customers, we see a message saying, "Your order will arrive in 10 minutes." The delivery rider, however, sees a different message: "You have 10 minutes to make it happen." Those are two very different experiences.

### The GTA Comparison

The situation reminded me of GTA. In some GTA missions, you try to save one person while creating absolute chaos on the road. Cars fly, people run, traffic gets destroyed, and the game simply tells you to get there quickly. Real life isn't GTA. There is no restart button, no respawn and no magical repair shop that fixes your bike for free after an accident.

A delivery rider might be trying to earn ₹30–₹50, but one bad decision on the road can result in thousands of rupees in repairs, medical expenses and lost working days. That ₹30 delivery can suddenly become a very expensive delivery.

### And It's Not Only About the Delivery Rider

There is another side to this problem. Rushed deliveries can also cause damage to other people's vehicles. A rider may cut across traffic, hit someone's mirror, scratch a car, knock over a parked bike or cause another vehicle to crash while trying to get through traffic. Identifying the rider can be difficult, and even if the person is stopped, recovering money for the damage can become another headache.

The person who caused the damage may have been working under pressure to complete an order, while the person whose vehicle was damaged is left dealing with the consequences. In the end, nobody really wins.

### Is the Problem the Rider?

I don't think the delivery rider is the villain here. Most riders are simply trying to earn a living. If the system rewards speed, people will naturally try to become faster. If an app promises customers delivery within a very short time, that pressure has to go somewhere, and it often reaches the person riding the bike.

So perhaps the bigger question is whether we, as customers, should continue demanding this level of speed.

### We Created the Demand

Instant delivery didn't become popular because companies forced us to use it. We liked it. We ordered groceries because we didn't want to walk to the shop, ordered snacks because we didn't want to leave home and ordered things late at night because we wanted them immediately. Over time, "10-minute delivery" started to feel normal.

Maybe we should occasionally ask ourselves whether we really need something within 10 minutes. If we can wait 30 minutes, why shouldn't we? If we can walk to a nearby shop, why shouldn't we? If we can buy something on our way home tomorrow, why do we need someone to rush through traffic tonight?

### Convenience Has a Cost

Technology has made our lives incredibly convenient, but convenience doesn't make the cost disappear. Sometimes, the cost simply moves somewhere else. It can appear as pressure on delivery workers, risky riding, traffic violations, vehicle damage, accidents, stress, pollution and more vehicles on already crowded roads.

We may save 20 minutes, but someone else may be risking much more than that.

### What Can We Do?

I'm not saying we should completely stop using delivery services overnight. I use them too. However, perhaps we can change how we use them. Before placing an instant-delivery order, we can ask ourselves, "Do I actually need this right now?" If the answer is no, we can wait, choose normal delivery when available or buy from a nearby store when possible.

Most importantly, we shouldn't complain when a delivery takes longer because the rider is driving safely. I'd rather receive my food 15 minutes late than receive a notification saying that the person delivering it met with an accident.

### Maybe We Should Slow Down

Technology has made almost everything faster, but faster isn't always better. Sometimes the most advanced thing we can do is simply wait.

The next time we order something for instant delivery, let's remember that there is a human being on the other side of that notification. They don't have a respawn button or infinite health, and their bike doesn't magically repair itself after an accident. Maybe saving 10 minutes isn't worth asking someone else to risk their life.

This is just my perspective after witnessing that accident.
